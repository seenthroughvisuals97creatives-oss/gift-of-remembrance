About A Gift of Remembrance

A space to pause. A space to listen. A space to remember. Not another place to become someone else, but a moment to meet yourself with greater presence.

How our feelings touch our health

  • Putting a feeling into words has been shown in brain-scan studies to quiet the amygdala, the part of the brain that sounds the alarm. This is why the session begins by asking you to name what you feel.
  • Long stretches of stress are linked in research with weaker immune responses. How we feel and how our body copes are connected.
  • People who tend to be optimistic have been found to have a lower risk of heart problems and early death. This is an association, not a promise.
  • Positive feelings are thought to widen what we notice and what we can do. Over time that builds lasting strengths such as friendships and resilience.
  • None of this means painful feelings are a failure. They are part of being alive. Awareness helps us decide how much room to give them.
Where this comes from
  • Lieberman et al. (2007). Putting feelings into words. Psychological Science.
  • Segerstrom and Miller (2004). Psychological stress and the human immune system: a meta-analytic study of 30 years of inquiry. Psychological Bulletin.
  • Rozanski et al. (2019). Association of optimism with cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Network Open.
  • Fredrickson (2001). The role of positive emotions in positive psychology: the broaden-and-build theory. American Psychologist.
